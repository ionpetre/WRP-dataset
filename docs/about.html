A short guide to the evidence

What do the survey answers tell us?

The World Risk Poll records what people worry about and which harmful experiences they report. Understanding those questions is the first step towards reading our findings.

A global survey about everyday safety

Lloyd’s Register Foundation created the World Risk Poll with Gallup to learn how people experience and perceive risks to their safety. It includes nationally representative surveys, including in places where other safety evidence is limited. The 2025 wave contains 143,459 interviews in 140 countries and areas. The four datasets used here were collected in 2019, 2021, 2023 and 2025.

The wider Poll covers more than our clustering questions. Our study concentrates on combinations of worry and reported harm. Readers can explore the broader evidence in the official data and documentation, and read Gallup’s account of the Poll’s purpose.

Worry and harm answer different questions

Worry records a person’s concern about a risk. The three response levels are not worried, somewhat worried and very worried. Harm records an experience, either to the respondent or to someone they personally know. Both experiences can be reported together.

We keep those two recipients separate. Harm to someone in a person’s network is not the same as harm to that person. A report that both experienced harm contributes to both indicators.

Neither answer alone gives the probability that the respondent will experience future harm. A highly worried person is not necessarily at greater measured risk, and a person reporting little worry can still have experienced harm.

What is a cluster?

A cluster is a group of respondents whose combinations of answers are similar under our analysis. A short name highlights a characteristic pattern. It does not describe every answer from every member, and it is not a diagnosis or a permanent identity.

For example, “fewer very-high-worry answers” does not mean “no worry” or “safe.” The detailed explorer shows the complete answer distributions, so readers can see what each summary keeps and leaves out.

Why compare several surveys?

A convincing pattern in one sample may change in another. We therefore analysed each wave independently and looked for combinations that recurred. We also repeated the fitting on different subsets of respondents and countries to see which findings persisted.

The later surveys supported a recurring seven-cluster description with four clearer components. A worry-only analysis gave an especially consistent, simpler grouping. The 2019 comparison did not support the same shared classification.

Why does 2019 need separate treatment?

The question sets changed. The 2019 data do not provide the same traffic-worry question or the later distinction between personal and known-person harm. We therefore compare five shared worries across all four years, and use the fuller worry–harm analysis only for 2021, 2023 and 2025.

Forcing different questions into the same set of inputs could produce differences that reflect measurement rather than a change in people’s concerns.

Who do the percentages describe?

We use respondents who answered all questions needed for that comparison. We do not replace missing answers with “no worry” or “no harm.” People with incomplete answers remain outside that clustering, so the findings need not describe them equally well.

Within a country, we preserve the survey weights. In comparisons across countries, every country receives equal influence. A large country therefore does not dominate the summary merely because it has a larger population.

For that reason, “30%” in our combined results should be read as an equal-country summary of eligible respondents, rather than “30% of the world’s population.” The detailed pages identify the population used for each result.

What does a change over time mean?

We distinguish two questions. First, do independently learned group definitions agree? Second, how common is one unchanged definition in each year? The latter comparison applies the 2025 model to earlier survey answers in the same countries.

The surveys interview different people. A changing group share does not tell us who moved between groups. It also does not establish the cause of the difference.
