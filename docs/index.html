What makes people feel unsafe, and how does that relate to the harm they experience? The World Risk Poll gives people around the world a voice on these questions. We use its answers to study which combinations of concerns recur over time.

Meet the World Risk Poll

The World Risk Poll (WRP) is a global survey from Lloyd’s Register Foundation, conducted by Gallup. It asks people about threats to their safety, how worried they feel, and the serious harm they or someone they know have experienced. It reaches countries where other evidence on everyday safety can be scarce.

Its value is the view it provides from people’s own lives. Accident records can tell us about recorded events; the Poll also tells us what people fear and which experiences they report. This evidence can help researchers, public authorities and organisations working on safety understand concerns that national averages may overlook. Read about the Poll’s purpose and origins.

These figures describe the original survey. Our comparisons use the respondents and countries with the answers required for each analysis. See the official data and survey documentation.

Our contribution: an average worry score can hide different concerns. We ask which worries and harmful experiences occur together, whether those combinations recur in another wave, and what they reveal about differences between countries and years.

Five clusters recur across 2021, 2023 and 2025. Each one is recovered consistently when we repeat the analysis. The same five answer rules describe the clusters in all three surveys with at least 80% precision.

Which parts of the Poll did we study?

The World Risk Poll asks people about risks in everyday life. Our main analysis combines worry about food, water, crime, severe weather, traffic and mental health with reports of harm to the respondent or to someone they personally know. Work-related harm is included too.

We compared the 2021, 2023 and 2025 surveys using the same questions. The 2019 survey did not ask all of these questions in comparable form. These surveys interview different people, so they show population patterns rather than individual journeys.

The questions behind the atlas

Which combinations of concerns does one overall score miss? Do those combinations recur in another survey? Can we then compare countries or years using a definition that stays the same?

Clusters bring together respondents with similar combinations of answers. Comparing several surveys shows which combinations recur and how reliably their boundaries can be recovered. They summarize answers, rather than defining fixed kinds of people.

Five clusters of worry and reported harm

We fitted each wave separately using the same encoding. Grouping traffic, crime and severe-weather worry together gave a more consistent result across surveys. The five clusters summarize distinctive answer patterns, and the rules below give an approximate way to describe each group.

Reading the rules. “Not very worried” includes not worried and somewhat worried. V counts very-worried answers across the six worry questions. K counts domains with reported harm to someone known, including work and answers reporting harm to both self and someone known.

Precision: among people meeting a rule, the share assigned to that cluster. Coverage: among people assigned to the cluster, the share meeting its rule. A respondent can meet more than one rule or none.

What this can help us understand

These summaries make it easier to see which concerns accompany one another and to compare the frequency of the same combination across countries or years. They also show why a single worry score or a country average can miss useful distinctions.

The clusters describe survey answers. They do not diagnose people, predict who will be harmed, or establish why concerns differ. Missing answers, country coverage and how people interpret the questions all matter. We report the less reliable parts alongside the clearer findings.
