Four clusters recur when we look at worry alone

We grouped answers to the same five questions about food, water, crime, severe weather and mental health. The same four patterns appeared in 2019, 2021, 2023 and 2025.

  • No crime worryMembers say they are not worried about crime. They can still have other concerns.
  • Crime worry without water worryMembers are at least somewhat worried about crime and say they are not worried about water.
  • Crime and water worry without mental-health worryMembers are at least somewhat worried about both crime and water and say they are not worried about mental health.
  • Crime, water and mental-health worryMembers are at least somewhat worried about crime, water and mental health.

How we assign the four worry-only clusters

A “yes” means the answer was somewhat worried or very worried. The rule applies to respondents who answered all five questions.

  1. Worried about crime? If no, assign No crime worry (C1). Otherwise continue.
  2. Worried about water? If no, assign Crime worry without water worry (C0). Otherwise continue.
  3. Worried about mental health? If no, assign Crime and water worry without mental-health worry (C3). If yes, assign Crime, water and mental-health worry (C2).

The five worry answers reproduce the same grouping in all four surveys. The three decisions give the exact assignments on every complete answer pattern.

How often did the result recur under refitting?

The decisions reproduced 798 of 800 refits over two confirmation rounds. The lowest 10th-percentile ARI and cluster Jaccard were both 1.00, with a lowest individual refit comparison ARI of 0.422.

The harm cluster cuts across all four worry-only groups

Harm to people they know draws members from all four worry-only clusters in every wave. The group with crime, water and mental-health worry contributes the most, at 61–66%. Looking in the other direction, 20–22% of that worry-only group belongs to the harm cluster.

Each bar shows 100% of the harm cluster in that wave. Both assignments use the same complete respondents, with equal weight across 92 development countries. Displayed percentages are rounded.

The four worry groups each contribute members to the harm cluster in every survey. The mix is similar across years. That tells us that asking about harm adds information about experiences shared by people with different worries.

How we tested the redistribution

We used the same 2025 answers for the models from every wave. The 2021 and 2023 overlap tables were each within two percentage points of the 2025 table in total variation. We also tested whether we could recover the worry-only groups by merging two worry–harm clusters. We chose the merge and label match that gave the highest agreement on 2025 answers. With that choice fixed, agreement across the three surveys was 48–51%.

What the harm questions add

A lookup based on the six worry answers reproduced 89–91% of the worry–harm labels overall. It found fewer than 1% of the members of the harm cluster. Applying the existing harm rule first raised that share to 72–75%. The rule asks for harm to someone known in at least five domains, or in exactly four with at least some water worry. These checks use the same development surveys.

Why the rules pick out different people

  • No crime worry checks one answer. Few very high worries checks that no domain other than food is rated very worried. The people selected by those descriptions have a weighted overlap of 0.46, measured by the intersection divided by the union.
  • Crime, water and mental-health worry requires at least some worry in those domains. The Mental-health worry rule in the main result requires very high mental-health worry, water below very worried, and harm to someone known in at most five domains.

The two analyses also use different inputs and fitting procedures. The matched test below keeps the worry questions and number of clusters the same.
